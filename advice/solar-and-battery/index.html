Combining a heat pump with solar panels and a battery

Solar panels and a battery can supply some of the electricity a heat pump uses, and the two technologies coexist well, but they do not solve the same problem. Solar generation is highest in summer while heating demand is highest in winter, so the honest way to plan the combination is around hot water and around when electricity is bought, rather than around eliminating a winter bill.

Three different technologies, three different jobs

There are three separate things people mean by “solar and battery”, and they behave differently.

Solar PV

Generates electricity from daylight: most in the middle of long summer days, considerably less in winter, nothing at night. Output is affected by roof orientation, pitch, shading and the time of year.

Battery storage

Does not generate anything. It shifts electricity in time: storing surplus solar for later, or charging from the grid during a cheaper tariff period and discharging when electricity costs more. For a heat pump, the second use is often the more relevant one.

Solar thermal

A different technology again, using panels to heat water directly. It can contribute to hot water, but it competes with the heat pump for the same cylinder and adds hydraulic complexity, so combining the two needs care.

The seasonal mismatch, stated plainly

In December, when a heat pump is working hardest, a domestic solar array in West Yorkshire produces a small fraction of its summer output. Anyone implying that solar will cover winter heating is not being straight with you.

Where the combination earns its place is more specific: covering hot water for much of the year, supplying a share of the heat pump’s electricity in the shoulder seasons, and using a battery to move consumption into cheaper tariff periods.

Why it matters for the outcome in your home

The practical consequence is one of sequencing and electrical capacity rather than heating performance.

The heat pump’s design does not change because you have solar. The heat loss calculation, the emitters, the flow temperature and the cylinder are all decided by the building. Solar changes where some of the electricity comes from, not how much heat the house needs.

What does change is the electrical picture. A heat pump is a significant load, and solar PV, a battery and an EV charger are all further loads or sources on the same consumer unit and incoming supply. Adding them without a plan is how households end up with an overloaded board or a main fuse that is too small.

There is also a control question. Directing surplus solar to the heat pump or the cylinder requires the two systems to communicate, or at least to be scheduled coherently. Two well-installed systems that ignore each other will still work; they simply will not cooperate.

What Airwarm checks and does about it

Airwarm’s Home Energy Assessment asks whether you are interested in solar or battery integration. Answering yes is useful even if the solar is years away, because it changes what the survey plans for.

The survey records the electrical supply, the existing consumer unit and any existing or planned generation and storage, and identifies where the network operator would need to be notified. Electrical work is carried out by appropriately qualified regional contractors working to Airwarm’s design and coordination.

Where solar is planned but not yet installed, Airwarm will design the heat pump installation so the later work does not mean undoing the first: cable routes, spare capacity, sensible positioning of the cylinder and controls, and a note of what the future installer will need.

Airwarm will also tell you where the combination does not stack up. A heavily shaded roof, a north-facing main elevation, a small roof area or a conservation restriction on visible panels are all real constraints, and the practical route forward may be a battery on a time-of-use tariff without solar, or nothing beyond the heat pump.

Common misconceptions, stated fairly

“Solar panels will run my heat pump.” They will contribute, mostly outside the heating season. They will not cover midwinter heating from a domestic roof in West Yorkshire.

“A battery makes the heat pump more efficient.” It does not touch efficiency. It changes when electricity is drawn and at what price, which may be worth having, but it is a tariff effect rather than an efficiency one.

“A solar diverter and a heat pump work well together.” A diverter sends surplus solar to an immersion heater. That is direct electric heating, which is far less efficient than using the heat pump to heat the same water. Sending surplus to the heat pump is usually better where the equipment supports it, and it is worth a specific conversation rather than a default.

“Solar thermal plus a heat pump is the best of both.” It can work, but it means two heat sources sharing a cylinder, more controls and more that can go wrong. A considered decision, not an add-on.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need solar to make a heat pump worthwhile?

No. They are independent decisions. A heat pump is a heating system; solar is a generation system.

Will my existing solar installation need changing?

Usually not, but the survey will check the electrical arrangement, the total load and whether the inverter and any battery can be scheduled alongside the heat pump.

Will I need my electricity supply upgraded?

It depends on the existing supply, the main fuse rating and the total load once everything is added. This is checked at survey, and where a network operator application is needed Airwarm will say so early rather than late.

Does a battery mean the heating keeps working in a power cut?

Only if the battery system is specifically configured for backup supply, which is a separate design feature and not a standard one. A heat pump is a substantial load, so backup running is not a small ask.
