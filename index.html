Smart heating

A heat pump works best when the whole system is designed to run at a lower temperature. Done properly, rooms hold a steady, even warmth rather than swinging between hot and cold.

What it depends on: heat loss calculated room by room, and emitters that can deliver that heat at a lower flow temperature.

Lower running costs

A heat pump moves heat rather than burning fuel, so it delivers more heat than the electricity it uses. What that means for your bills depends on your home, your tariff and how the system is set up — which is exactly what a survey is for.

We will not put a savings figure on this page. Anyone who does it before seeing your house is guessing.

Greener futures

No gas, oil or LPG burnt in the home, no flue and no combustion products. As the electricity grid gets cleaner, so does the heating.

There is no gas safety check on a heat pump, but there is still an annual service. It keeps the efficiency where the design put it.

Suitability first

A good heat-pump installation begins with the home, not the product

Airwarm checks the building, emitters, hot-water requirements, controls and practical installation details before making a recommendation.

That order matters. A heat pump sized for a home that has not been assessed is the single most common reason people end up disappointed. Too large a unit cycles and costs more to run than it should; too small a unit cannot hold the house at temperature on the coldest few days of the year. Neither problem is visible on a quotation.

So the first question is never “which heat pump?” It is how much heat your house loses, room by room, and what your existing radiators or underfloor pipework can actually give out at a lower water temperature. If your property needs work first, we will say so and explain why. If a heat pump is not the right next step for you today, we will tell you that too.

Read our guide to what actually decides suitability, or start with the assessment and let us look at your answers.

What we look at

  • Building fabric and insulation
  • Radiator or underfloor emitter capacity
  • Hot-water demand and cylinder space

  • Controls and how you actually use the house
  • Electrical supply and practical pipe routes

Not every home, and not every home today

Some properties are not ready for a heat pump, and a few are not suitable at all. That is a statement about a building’s condition today rather than a verdict on it, and there is nearly always something worth doing either way. You will get the reason and the practical options, not a brush-off.

Quick answers

Will a heat pump work in my house?

Most homes can be heated well by a heat pump, but not every home today and not every home without changes. It depends on how much heat your house loses, whether the radiators can deliver that heat at a lower water temperature, whether there is a sensible position for the outdoor unit and room for a hot water cylinder, and whether the electrical supply is adequate. The Home Energy Assessment is a short first check, and a survey gives the real answer.

Do heat pumps work in cold weather?

Yes. Air source heat pumps extract heat from outside air well below freezing. Efficiency reduces as it gets colder, and demand rises at the same time, so consumption is highest in the coldest weather. The system is sized for a cold design day rather than an average one.

How much does a heat pump installation cost?

Airwarm is not publishing prices ahead of its trading launch, and a figure quoted without a survey would be a guess. What determines the cost is the size of the system, how many emitters need changing, the cylinder and its position, the pipework and electrical work required, and access. The proposal sets all of it out itemised.

Will a heat pump reduce my energy bills?

That depends on your building, your tariff, the efficiency the system actually achieves and how you use it, so Airwarm will not put a figure on it. What Airwarm will do is explain the things that determine running cost and design the system to run at the lowest useful flow temperature.
