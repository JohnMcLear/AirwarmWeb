Heat pumps designed around your home

An air source heat pump can heat a house very well — but only when the rest of the system is designed to suit it. This page explains what one actually does, where it works well, and what usually needs checking first.

What an air source heat pump does

It takes heat out of the outside air and moves it into your home’s heating and hot water. It does not burn anything. Because it moves heat rather than making it, it delivers several units of heat for each unit of electricity it uses.

The trade-off is temperature. A gas boiler happily pushes water round at 70 °C or more. A heat pump is at its most efficient at much lower flow temperatures, so the radiators or underfloor pipework have to be able to give out enough heat at that lower temperature. That single fact drives most of what a survey looks at.

Who this suits

  • Homeowners who want the heating designed for the property
  • People willing to consider radiator or fabric improvements where they are needed
  • Households replacing oil, LPG, storage heaters or an ageing boiler
  • Renovations, where the heating can be planned alongside the building work

If you want the cheapest possible swap with nothing else changed, Airwarm is probably not the right choice — and we would rather say that now.

Honest suitability

The five things that decide whether a heat pump will work well

1. Building fabric

How much heat the house loses sets everything else: the size of the heat pump, the size of the radiators and the running cost. Loft insulation, wall construction, draughts and glazing all matter. Older solid-wall stone housing — common across Bradford and Airedale — is not automatically unsuitable, but it does need assessing rather than assuming.

2. Radiators or underfloor heating

Emitters sized for a boiler are often too small for lower flow temperatures. Some rooms may need a larger radiator; others may be fine as they are. This is measured room by room, not guessed, and it is the most common reason a system underperforms when it is done badly.

3. Hot water

Heat pumps heat hot water into a cylinder rather than on demand like a combination boiler. That needs somewhere sensible to put a well-insulated cylinder, and enough capacity for how your household actually uses hot water.

4. The outdoor unit

The unit needs a position with free air flow, sensible pipe and condensate routes, tolerable noise for you and your neighbours, and safe access for servicing. Terraces with no rear access, tight side passages and shared yards all need thinking about early.

5. Controls and how you live

Heat pumps prefer steady, gentle running to short blasts. Weather compensation, sensible setback and a system that has been balanced make a real difference. So does knowing how the household actually uses the house.

And the electrical supply

The incoming supply, consumer unit and cabling route all need checking. Electrical work is carried out by vetted qualified contractors where it falls outside our own scope.

Common concerns, answered plainly

Will it keep my house warm in a Yorkshire winter?

Yes, if it is sized and designed for your house. Heat pumps are designed against a local winter design temperature, and they work in far colder climates than ours. The risk is not the weather; it is a system that was never properly sized.

Do I need to replace all my radiators?

Usually not all of them. Some rooms need a larger emitter, some are already adequate. It is worked out room by room during the survey, and you see the results.

Are they noisy?

A modern outdoor unit is generally a low, steady sound rather than an intrusive one, but position matters a great deal — distance from windows and boundaries, and reflecting surfaces. Noise is assessed as part of siting, not treated as an afterthought.

What about my hot water?

A cylinder is needed. If you currently have a combination boiler and no airing cupboard, finding a sensible cylinder location becomes one of the main practical questions for your property.

What will it cost, and what will I save?

We are not going to put a figure on this page. Installation cost depends on the property, the emitter work and the hot-water arrangement, and running cost depends on your home, your tariff and how the system is set up. Anyone quoting you a number before seeing your house is guessing. You will get real figures after a survey, in writing, with the assumptions shown.

What about grants and funding?

Support schemes exist, but eligibility depends on the certification route of the installer as well as on your property. Airwarm is finalising that route, so we are not going to describe funding you may or may not be able to use. Ask us by e-mail and we will tell you exactly where things stand.
