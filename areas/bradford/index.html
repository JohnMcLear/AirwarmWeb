Heat pumps for Bradford’s stone-built housing

Bradford is one of the three areas Airwarm is starting with, and it is the one where the local building tradition matters most to the answer. Solid stone walls, tight terraced streets and real altitude all change how a heat pump has to be designed.

What Bradford homes are usually like

A large share of Bradford’s housing predates 1919 and was built from local sandstone. Those walls are typically solid rather than cavity, and often thick — which means they cannot be insulated by injecting a cavity, and any fabric improvement has to be internal or external. It also means the walls behave differently from brick: slower to warm, slower to cool.

Around the city centre and the inner districts the stock is dominated by through terraces and back-to-backs, in long rows, frequently on a slope. Further out — Idle, Thornton, Wibsey, Clayton and the outer villages — there is far more interwar and post-war semi-detached and detached housing, much of it cavity-built and considerably more straightforward.

Altitude is the factor people forget. A good deal of the district sits well above the valley floors, and the higher, more exposed parts of Bradford are colder and windier than a national average would suggest. That feeds directly into the winter design temperature a system has to cope with, and it is a reason not to accept a design produced from a postcode lookup alone.

Practical considerations here

  • Solid walls. Not a barrier, but heat loss must be calculated rather than assumed from a standard table.
  • Rear access. Many terraces open onto a shared yard, a ginnel or nothing at all, which limits where an outdoor unit can go.
  • Boundary distances. On a terrace, your unit is close to a neighbour’s wall or window, so noise assessment matters more.
  • Cylinder space. Older terraces often have a small, awkward first floor and no obvious airing cupboard.
  • Conservation areas. The district has a number of them, including Little Germany, where what is permitted externally is more restricted.
  • Parking and materials. Steep, narrow terraced streets make delivery and working space a genuine planning question.

Why we start with the fabric in Bradford

In a solid stone terrace the honest answer is sometimes “insulation first”. A heat pump sized for an uninsulated solid-wall house will be larger, more expensive and more costly to run than one sized for the same house after sensible fabric work. We would rather tell you that at the assessment stage than sell you the bigger unit.

That does not mean stone terraces are unsuitable. Plenty are good candidates, particularly where the loft has been done properly and the radiators are reasonably generous. It means the order of work matters, and that is a conversation worth having early.

Bradford questions

Can a solid stone terrace really have a heat pump?

Yes, plenty can. What changes is the amount of care needed in the calculation and, sometimes, the order of work — insulation and radiators before the heat pump rather than at the same time. The survey gives you the numbers rather than an opinion.

Where would the outdoor unit go on a terraced house?

Usually the rear elevation or a rear yard where there is one, sometimes a side passage. It needs free air flow, a sensible condensate route and enough distance from windows and boundaries. On some terraces there is no workable position, and that is one of the first things we check rather than the last.

Do you cover the whole Bradford district?

Bradford, Shipley and the areas between them are our starting point. If you are further out in the district, e-mail us and we will tell you honestly whether we can get to you properly rather than taking the booking and hoping.

My street is in a conservation area. Does that stop it?

Not necessarily, but it changes what is permitted externally and may affect where the unit can be seen from. It needs checking early rather than assumed, and we will tell you what we find.
