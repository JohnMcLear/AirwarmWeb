Heat pumps in Leeds, where no two streets are the same

Leeds has a wider range of housing than anywhere else Airwarm covers — back-to-back terraces, interwar semis, suburban detached houses, city-centre flats and new estates, sometimes within a mile of each other. The right answer varies just as much.

Four different Leeds, four different answers

The inner terraces. Leeds retains an unusually large number of back-to-back terraces — houses that share three walls with their neighbours and have no rear elevation at all. They are mostly brick rather than stone, and they are compact and relatively easy to heat. The difficulty is physical: with only a front elevation, finding a legitimate position for an outdoor unit is the first question, not a detail. Through terraces in the same districts usually have more options.

The interwar and post-war suburbs. Large parts of Leeds are 1930s onwards semi-detached and detached housing, much of it cavity-built with a garden and a side or rear elevation going spare. These are often the most straightforward properties we look at: cavity insulation, reasonable radiators and a sensible place to put the unit.

North Leeds and the larger houses. Bigger detached and semi-detached property means a bigger heat demand but usually more room for a cylinder, more choice of unit position, and more scope to run at genuinely low flow temperatures if the radiators are up to it.

Flats and apartments. Leasehold flats and converted buildings bring in the freeholder, the lease, shared external walls and communal areas. An individual air source heat pump is often not practical, and we will say so early rather than running you through a survey to reach the same conclusion.

Practical considerations here

  • Back-to-backs. No rear elevation. Unit siting is the first thing to establish, and sometimes there is no answer.
  • Leasehold and shared buildings. Permission, not just physics. Worth checking before anything else.
  • Suburban semis. Frequently good candidates, particularly where the cavity and loft have been done.
  • Combination boilers. Common in the suburbs, so a cylinder position is often the main practical change.
  • Newer estates. Better fabric and often smaller heat demand, but sometimes radiators sized very tightly to a boiler.
  • Travel and access. City-centre and inner-ring parking affects how a working week is planned.

Why the assessment matters more in a mixed city

In an area with one dominant housing type you can make reasonable assumptions. In Leeds you cannot. Two homes on adjoining streets can need completely different sizing, different emitter work and different siting, and the only honest way to tell them apart is to ask about the actual property.

That is what the online assessment is for. It is a few minutes, it asks for no photographs, and it will tell you whether it is worth going any further.

Leeds questions

I live in a back-to-back. Is it possible at all?

Sometimes, and sometimes not. The heat demand is often modest, which helps; the obstacle is having only one external elevation to work with, plus condensate and noise close to a pavement or neighbours. It is worth asking, and it is worth asking first rather than last.

I have a combination boiler and no airing cupboard.

Very common in the Leeds suburbs. A heat pump needs a cylinder, so finding a sensible position for one becomes a main design question — loft, understairs, a utility area or a reconfigured bathroom are all possibilities depending on the house.

Do you cover all of Leeds?

We are starting with the parts of Leeds we can reach and service properly alongside Bradford and Shipley. E-mail us with your postcode area and you will get a straight answer either way.

My house was built in the last ten years. Is that easier?

Usually, because the fabric is better and the heat demand is lower. Watch the radiators though: emitters sized precisely for a high-temperature boiler can still be too small for low-temperature operation, so they get measured like anyone else’s.
